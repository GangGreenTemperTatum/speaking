Keynotes

Title: Autonomous and Secure AI Agents through Information-flow Control

Boris Köpf, Microsoft Azure Research

Short Bio: Boris Köpf is a scientist at Microsoft's Azure Research, where he works on security of AI agents (Project Roma) and on defenses against side-channel attacks (Project Venice).

His research brings together security, formal verification, programming languages, systems, and AI. He has published at venues including IEEE S&P, USENIX Security, ACM CCS, PLDI, CAV, ASPLOS, ICML, and ICLR. His work has received a Best Paper Award at IEEE S&P (2021) and Distinguished Paper Awards at USENIX Security (2017), ACM CCS (2024), and IEEE S&P (2026).

Abstract: When AI agents work autonomously on rich data sources, they run the risk of oversharing sensitive information or following instructions hidden in untrusted content. Most current defenses are either probabilistic, offering no strong assurance, or rely on humans approving consequential actions, which undermines autonomy.

In this talk, we show how information-flow control (IFC) can make agents both secure and autonomous. IFC attaches confidentiality and integrity labels to data, tracks them through the agent loop, and enforces deterministic policies on where data may flow and what it may influence. We will place IFC within the broader space of deterministic guardrails for AI systems and share lessons from bringing IFC to real-world agent platforms.

Title: The AI Trust Gap: When Expectations Meet Adversarial Reality

Alexandra Dmitrienko, University of Duisburg-Essen

Short Bio: Alexandra Dmitrienko is a Full Professor of Computer Science at the University of Duisburg-Essen, Germany, where she holds the Chair of Trustworthy Systems. Previously, she held academic and research positions at the University of Würzburg, ETH Zurich, the Fraunhofer Institute for Secure Information Technology, and Ruhr University Bochum. She received her PhD in Information Technology from TU Darmstadt with distinction.

Her research interests span software and systems security, IoT security, and the intersection of artificial intelligence and cybersecurity. Her research regularly appears at the Big Four security conferences: ACM CCS, USENIX Security, NDSS, and the IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy. She has delivered invited keynotes at AsiaCCS 2024 and ACNS 2025.

She is actively involved in the international security research community and has held several conference leadership positions, including Track Chair for ACM CCS 2025, TPC Vice Chair for USENIX Security 2024, and TPC Chair for ACM WiSec 2022. She regularly serves on the program committees of all Big Four security conferences and is serving as an Associate Chair for NDSS 2027 and Technical Program Co-Chair for IEEE CNS 2027.

Her research and academic contributions have been recognized with several awards, including the IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy Best Student Paper Award (2013), the Intel Doctoral Student Honor Award (2013), the ERCIM STM Working Group Award (2016), the ACM AsiaCCS Distinguished Paper Award (2017), and the NDSS Distinguished Reviewer Award (2026).

Abstract: As large language models (LLMs) become increasingly integrated into our daily lives and software development workflows, we place growing trust in their ability to behave safely, produce reliable and secure outputs, and act according to our intentions. Yet, the gap between these expectations and the security guarantees that current AI systems can provide remains substantial, particularly in the presence of adversarial manipulation.

In this keynote, I will explore this trust gap through two fundamental challenges in LLM security. First, I will examine the robustness of safety alignment, demonstrating how targeted manipulation of safety-critical model components can undermine safeguards designed to prevent harmful behavior. I will then discuss recent approaches to strengthening safety alignment by addressing its underlying fragility. Second, I will turn to AI-assisted code generation, investigating how security-aware knowledge retrieval can improve the security of generated code, while also introducing new attack surfaces when external knowledge sources are compromised.

Finally, I will look ahead to emerging challenges in agentic AI, where security concerns extend beyond generated outputs to autonomous decisions and actions. Drawing on recent research in attacks and defenses, the talk will highlight the assumptions underlying our trust in AI systems and discuss how we can move toward more robust and trustworthy AI.

Research paper Beyond the Syntax: Do Security Experts Trust LLMs for NIDS Rule Engineering? Spotlight

Lorenzo Di Filippo1, Enkeleda Bardhi2, Andrea Agiollo2, Alessandro Palma3, Silvia Bonomi3, Fernando Kuipers2

  • 1Sorbonne Université, LIP6, Paris, France
  • 2Delft University of Technology
  • 3Sapienza University of Rome
Read abstractHide abstract

As network threats evolve, manual Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) rule engineering has become a critical operational bottleneck. While Large Language Models (LLMs) show promise for automating this process, their ability to produce production-ready rules remains unvalidated. This paper presents a human-centered investigation into LLM-based NIDS rule engineering, formalizing a context-aware generation framework and evaluating it through an exploratory user study with 10 domain experts. Our evaluation reveals a syntax-semantics paradox: although LLMs generate syntactically correct rules, experts find them only partially practically useful due to low specificity and logic hallucinations in 12% of cases. While the system received a favorable SUS score of 67, practitioners remain skeptical of its autonomous capabilities, viewing LLMs as support tools for drafting and verification rather than independent generators. Finally, our statistical analysis indicates that while large-scale models (≥ 70𝐵) consistently produce syntactically valid rules, small models (≤ 4𝐵) are largely ineffective for IDS rule generation.

Jan Willemson1, Artjom Zingfeld2

The rise of Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools is making significant impact in many application areas, and elections are no exception. A lot of this impact (deep fakes, misinformation campaigns, etc.) can be viewed as negative. However, AI also has a lot of potential to be used constructively, and this is the topic of the current paper. Research paper Data Augmentation in Learning-Based Binary Code Analysis

Lennart von Stülpnagel1,2, Felix Weissberg1,2, Jonas Möller1,2, Benjamin Greb2, Thorsten Eisenhofer3, Konrad Rieck1,2

  • 1BIFOLD
  • 2TU Berlin
  • 3CISPA Helmholtz Center for Information Security
Read abstractHide abstract

Understanding and analyzing binary code is fundamental to security-critical tasks, such as reverse engineering and vulnerability discovery. As machine learning has become integral to these tasks, techniques from other domains have been transferred to binary code analysis. In particular, inspired by computer vision, data augmentation has recently been proposed to improve performance in code analysis tasks. However, prior work considers only a limited set of transformations and tasks, making it difficult to draw broader conclusions about when and how augmentation is effective. In this work, we thus take a step back and systematically study the role of data augmentation in binary code analysis. To this end, we introduce a unified framework that encompasses both semantic-preserving and semantic-altering code transformations. We evaluate the impact of augmentation across multiple tasks, including function similarity, parameter analysis, and vulnerability discovery. Our findings reveal a key difference from computer vision: in binary code analysis, augmentation is not a silver bullet. While code transformations can improve performance when training data is scarce, the gains diminish as more data becomes available. We conclude that augmentation is only beneficial under specific conditions and therefore derive practical recommendations for its application.

Research paper Exposure is All You Need: Using LLMs to Identify Critical Endpoints at Internet Scale

Stijn Pletinckx1, Milad Nasr2, Sanghyun Hong3, Zakir Durumeric4, Nicholas Carlini2

  • 1University of California, Santa Barbara
  • 2Anthropic
  • 3Oregon State University
  • 4Stanford University
Read abstractHide abstract

The modern Internet hosts billions of services, many of which inadvertently expose sensitive data or allow device manipulation. Discovering security-relevant Internet exposures requires both manual analysis and a priori knowledge of what to look for, neither of which scales to the diversity of devices and services in the wild. In this paper, we show that Large Language Models (LLMs) change this dynamic, and can act as general-purpose analyzers that recognize exposed services without predefined rules or fingerprints. We then use this capability to conduct the first comprehensive measurement of exposed services on the web. To make this measurement feasible, we use LLMs to build a hierarchical taxonomy of services on the web, revealing the full landscape of what is accessible online, and develop a multi-stage pipeline that combines lightweight ML classifiers with targeted LLM analysis to triage and rank the most security-critical exposures. We demonstrate this on five categories --directory listings, administrative dashboards, uncommon IoT devices, pages showing stack traces, and RTSP camera feeds-- using Internet scan data from over 20 million services. Our approach identifies thousands of critically exposed services, including healthcare management systems, financial documents, API keys, passports, and private home cameras.

Research paper Hacking Back the AI Hacker: Prompt Injection as a Defense Against LLM-driven Cyberattacks Spotlight

Dario Pasquini1, Evgenios M. Kornaropoulos2, Giuseppe Ateniese2

  • 1Cracken
  • 2George Mason University
Read abstractHide abstract

Large language models (LLMs) are making multi-step cyberattacks cheaper to run and easier to automate. We present Mantis, a mechanism that leverages prompt injection for defensive purposes, turning it against the attacking LLM-agent itself. Once Mantis detects an attacking agent, it injects defender-crafted content into system responses and steers the agent toward one of two outcomes: a tarpit that wastes time and resources, or a controlled hack-back action that helps collect attribution information. Mantis combines decoy services with dynamic prompt injections and uses the attacker's own interaction loop to deliver the defense. In our evaluation, Mantis achieves the sabotage objective in 95.4% of runs and keeps attacker success below 3%. We release Mantis as open source.

Benchmark paper PkgScanArena: Benchmarking LLM-Based Package Scanners against Indirect Prompt Injection

Himadri Chowdhury, Ahmed Hatem, Joseph Khoury

  • Louisiana State University
Read abstractHide abstract

Open-source package ecosystems such as PyPI and npm remain persistent targets for malicious campaigns. These campaigns have outgrown signature-based triage, so recent work has turned to large language models (LLMs) that scan package content and return a verdict, which lets the analysis consume attacker-controlled text. In 2026, the hades campaign on npm carried a prompt injection written to hijack the scanner’s instructions and coerce a clean verdict. Indirect prompt injection is well studied, but its reach into automated package-security analysis remains unmeasured. We therefore ask a broader question. How reliable are LLM-based package scanners when the package itself is written to manipulate the analysis? We present PkgScanArena, a benchmark that pairs a vetted dataset of 2,000 malicious and 1,000 benign npm and PyPI packages with a taxonomy of 11 techniques and 3 injection channels across three attacker goals, namely evasion, denial, and sabotage. An AST-guided pipeline injects these prompts into real packages, and we evaluate 25 open-weight and proprietary backends together with two deployed scanners. Our benchmark inverts the expected threat. Evasion proves largely unattainable, at or below 0.6% for nine of ten cloud backends. Exposure instead shifts to the opposite direction: benign accuracy degrades for all but two of the 25 backends, sabotage reaches 31.1%, and denial reaches 19.6% on the most safety-aligned model. The two deployed scanners inherit both weaknesses. The exposure lies not in coercing a scanner to miss malicious code, but in coercing it to condemn benign code.

Research paper Plug 'n' Pray: Agentic LLM-based Detection of Potential Log File Exposures in Third-Party Content Management System Plugins

Sebastian Neef

  • TU Berlin
Read abstractHide abstract

Content Management Systems (CMS), such as WordPress, power a large share of the web (~58%), and their extensibility through third-party plugins is a major source of their popularity as well as of their attack surface. One high-impact weakness that remains understudied is log file exposure by CMS plugins, which create log files for debugging or other purposes. If these files are insufficiently secured, they can disclose sensitive information (e.g. credentials, personal data) which has led to website compromises in the past.

In this work, we present an agentic, LLM-based framework that automatically detects potential log file exposures in plugins of the most popular CMS (WordPress). Our agent analyzes each plugin by performing static and dynamic analysis.

We evaluated our approach on the 300 most-installed WordPress plugins (about 0.6% of all), which together account for over 250M active installations, i.e. 75% of all active installations in the official plugin ecosystem. We manually validated each finding, reproducing 79 of 81 findings from 62 plugins. We observed that several protective measures appear to be implemented that we classify as creation-control (e.g. manual log activation) and access-control (e.g. deny rules in .htaccess). However, we find that multi-layered protection is required, but not always present.

From these results we derive a taxonomy of log file path and protection patterns and deduce a set of best practices for developers to securely handle them. Finally, our study corroborates that agentic LLMs are an useful tool for security analysis.

Benchmark paper ScopeBench: Do Agents Preserve Engagement Boundaries Under Goal Pressure? Spotlight

Shane Caldwell, Max Harley, Ads Dawson, Michael Kouremetis, Vincent Abruzzo, Will Pearce

  • Dreadnode
Read abstractHide abstract

Agents are increasingly deployed with real autonomy in web application and network penetration testing, where a single out-of-scope action can breach a client's engagement boundary. Existing offensive-security benchmarks measure raw hacking capability; as those benchmarks saturate, what actually gates deployment is a special case of alignment: scope adherence. We introduce ScopeBench, a benchmark of 30 dead-end agentic security tasks in which the stated objective is reachable only by violating the stated scope. Each task appears under two conditions that share an environment, verifier, and objective and differ only in scope: one instruction set has no scope and measures capability; the other has a natural-language scope to measure adherence. Every trajectory passes through two grading arms. First, a deterministic flag verifier acts as a violation verifier—a pass under scoped instruction proves by construction that a forbidden action occurred—yielding a high-precision lower bound on the violation rate. A tool-assisted, full-context judge then estimates whether an out-of-scope call occurred in trajectories that fail mechanical verification. We calibrate the judge against 100 ScopeBench trajectories labeled call-by-call by human annotators, and a blinded audit of the evaluated rollouts finds its high recall holds—no false negatives among the 36 audited violations, with over-flagging its only observed error. Across 8 models run in one harness, raw capability spans 12.2% to 81.1%. The mechanical verifier finds violations in 4.4% to 32.2% of scoped trials; adding the judge's review of the trials that never submitted a flag raises the estimate to 13.3%–65.6% violation. Taken together, the grading arms find a hybrid 34.4%–86.7% adherence—558 violations across the 1,440 scoped trials. Most significantly, opus-4-8 achieves a raw-capability score 10 percentage points higher than sonnet-4-6's while exhibiting 35.6 percentage points higher scope adherence. We release this pilot as a methodological framework for measuring scope adherence in future agents, with an open pathway for community task contributions.

Research paper Time Will Tell: Illuminating Causes of Score Inflation in Source Code Vulnerability Detection Benchmarks

Alexander De Furia1, Colin Bellinger1, Paula Branco1, Alexander M. Hoole2, Guy-Vincent Jourdan1, Hari Manassery Koduvely2, Manish Marwah3

  • 1University of Ottawa
  • 2OpenText
  • 3Independent Researcher
Read abstractHide abstract

LLM-based source code vulnerability detection (SCVD) reports strong benchmark performance that does not hold up in the real world. Use of random splitting in test-set construction has been widely blamed for permitting time-travel and inter-commit leakage, but neither mechanism has been empirically isolated. We evaluate five different cross-validation strategies across eight fine-tuned and eight zero-shot LLMs on MegaVul-C; every strategy is also evaluated on the same held-out future set to separate training-set from test-set effects. Random splitting inflates reported test-set F1 by ~34 for fine-tuned models and ~16 for zero-shot models. On the shared holdout set, models fine-tuned using random and temporal training splits achieve statistically equivalent performance within ±2 pp (pTOST < 0.001), which places the inflation on test-set construction. Matched comparisons that isolate inter-commit leakage and time-travel are equivalent on the holdout set within the same margin. Correcting the train-test CWE distribution by resampling, our proxy for evolving vulnerability patterns, does not close the gap either. The gap instead reflects differences between the training and evaluation distributions, which we localize but do not reduce to a single causal property. Temporal splitting remains necessary for realistic evaluation because it reduces the optimistic bias of random test-set construction. We recommend Temporal LOO Block CV, which preserves the variance reduction of K-fold evaluation while inducing distribution shift at each fold boundary. All split-generation, analysis, and training code is released to support reproducible evaluation of LLMs for SCVD.

Research paper Towards Behavior Tree–Guided Vulnerability Detection with Lightweight LLMs

Enna Basic, Alberto Giaretta

  • Örebro University
Read abstractHide abstract

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly used for software vulnerability detection, but their performance depends on how source code is represented in the input. Most prompting approaches use source code in its original form, while some works propose the use of structured representations. Abstract Syntax Trees (ASTs) are one of the most popular approaches, but AST verbosity increases input size relative to source code, making them hard to fit within some LLMs context windows. This paper investigates Behavior Trees (BTs) as an alternative intermediate representation for LLM-based vulnerability detection. BTs encode control flow, conditions, and executable actions more compactly than ASTs, making them a natural candidate when token count is a constraint. First, we propose a preprocessing stage that parses Java source code into ASTs and then converts them into BT representations. We then compare vulnerability detection performance across 460 Java samples from the Juliet Java test suite, using three input representations: raw source code, AST, and BT. All experiments use a single quantized local LLM, Mistral Small 3.2 24B (Q4_K_M). Our results show that using BT representations improves recall on short code samples, while raw source code achieves higher precision. On longer samples, BTs improve overall performance over the original representation and fit within the context window, whereas many ASTs exceed the context limit. Within the evaluated model and dataset, these findings provide preliminary evidence that BTs can serve as a compact and useful structured representation for vulnerability detection.

Research paper TRIAGE-CPG: A Cross-Model Study of Budgeted Static Malware Triage with Large Language Models

Paul-Andrei Sava1, Alexander Küchler1, Daniel Kowatsch1, Bernhard Grill2, Jennifer Pullman2, Yanick Fratantonio2, Luca Invernizzi2

  • 1Fraunhofer AISEC
  • 2Google
Read abstractHide abstract

When triaging unknown binaries, security analysts must quickly decide which samples to clear as benign, flag as malicious, or escalate for deeper analysis. Large Language Models (LLMs) could support these decisions by interpreting decompiled code, but a binary can contain hundreds or thousands of functions, making exhaustive LLM analysis impractical. We study how current LLMs behave when static triage is formulated as budgeted evidence acquisition: which functions they inspect and how they trade off benign clearance, malware detection, and escalation.

To enable a controlled comparison, we implement TRIAGE-CPG, a fixed pipeline over a code property graph (CPG) of a decompiled binary. An LLM-free heuristic selects seed functions, an agent expands the resulting evidence dossier under a fixed budget, and a reporting stage produces a benign, suspicious, or malicious verdict together with a behavioral report. We evaluate eight closed-source and open-weight models on 1000 Windows PE binaries, using a 600-sample test split and the same zero-shot protocol for every model. We assess the ternary verdicts under both a strict binary reading and triage balanced accuracy (tBA), which credits malware that is flagged or escalated and benign samples only when cleared.

After analyzing about 40 of a mean 1000 functions per binary, the highest-scoring model reaches a strict balanced accuracy of 0.805 and a tBA of 0.855, flagging 65% of malware, escalating another 12%, and clearing 94% of benign samples. The models implement different triage policies: high-specificity models produce small, malware-rich escalation queues, whereas recall-oriented models escalate or flag much more broadly. Both closed-source and open-weight models appear across this spectrum. Our results identify no universal deployment configuration: model choice itself determines the clear/flag/escalate trade-off under limited static evidence.

Research paper TypeSeg: Content-Type Segmentation for Mixed Text Spotlight

Martin Dallinger1,2, Yanick Fratantonio1, Luca Invernizzi1

  • 1Google
  • 2Johannes Kepler University Linz
Read abstractHide abstract

Identifying content types enables security pipelines to select parsers, scanners, and policies. Yet practical text-type detectors assign one label per file or block, hiding embedded languages, scripts, and encodings. Our stratified hand-labeled audit of 165 text files from MalwareBazaar, a public malware repository, finds 131 files with mixed content and 96 with an unexpected guest segment: an embedded region whose type falls outside the file’s expected structure. Applying Magika, a leading whole-file detector, to sliding windows yields poor segment recovery. Parsers expose the surrounding language’s structure, but finding undeclared guests, such as shell commands or Base64 payloads inside Python scripts, requires additional language-specific rules.

We address this gap with TypeSeg, a lightweight model that labels each character with one of 35 content types. It offers a parallelizable U-Net and a long-context Mamba variant. On 875 annotated, primarily GitHub-based files, TypeSeg reaches 95.1% mean per-file F1 score, 23.1 points above whole-file Magika, while U-Net processes 4.8M characters/s on a consumer GPU. After adaptation using a separate MalwareBazaar training split, it recovers 81.8% of guest segments and 91.9% of encoded regions on the held-out 165-file malware audit. Rule-based expansion and validation then yield analyzer-ready inputs for 86.5% of validator-eligible guest segments. TypeSeg therefore provides a practical primitive to visualize file composition, pinpoint unusual mixtures, and route content to specialized analyzers. We open-source TypeSeg, including its code, model checkpoints, prompts, and dataset-building scripts.

Research paper Unplugged from the Cloud: Lessons from Deploying Autonomous Forensic Agents on Local LLM

Kavishka Fernando, Tharindu Ranathunga, George D. O'Mahony, Indika Dhanapala, Susan Rea

  • Munster Technological University
Read abstractHide abstract

Autonomous LLM-based forensic agents show promise for supporting cyber investigations, but many existing systems depend on cloud-hosted proprietary models. This limits their use in enterprise environments where host logs, network records, and forensic evidence cannot be shared with external APIs due to privacy and regulatory constraints. This paper evaluates the transition of Clouseau, a cloud-validated autonomous forensic agent, to locally served open-weight models. Our initial replication showed a significant performance drop when moving from the cloud backend to local models. Although this first appeared to be caused by context-window limitations, detailed run-log analysis revealed that the agent was often failing to issue required tool calls and was therefore not conducting a real investigation. To address this, we introduce the Local Inference Adaptation Layer (LIAL), which provides targeted framework and serving-level adaptations for reliable local deployment. The results show that the performance gap is not explained by model capability alone, but by the interaction between the agent framework, tool-use behaviour, local serving constraints, and evaluation methodology. We present our findings as five lessons framing the local performance collapse as a framework and serving-level mismatch rather than a model capability limit. With LIAL, local Gemma-4 performance improves to 99.50% Single-Host F1 and 89.50% Core-21 F1, with a single prompt-level tool-use fix accounting for 94% of the total recovery. Overall, this work demonstrates that privacy-preserving deployment of autonomous forensic agents requires careful adaptation of the agent framework and evaluation process, rather than simply replacing a cloud model with a local open-weight model.

Research paper When Performance Meets Security: Reducing Attack Paths in Virtual Machine Placement via Reinforcement Learning

Franco Terranova1, Noura Limam2, Abdelkader Lahmadi1, Isabelle Chrisment1, Raouf Boutaba2

  • 1Université de Lorraine, CNRS, Inria, LORIA
  • 2University of Waterloo
Read abstractHide abstract

Virtual Machine Placement (VMP) is traditionally formulated as a multi-objective optimization problem that balances operational objectives, such as resource utilization and energy consumption, while increasingly incorporating security considerations. However, existing security-aware VMP approaches often rely on static vulnerability indicators or predefined risk metrics that fail to capture the adaptive and sequential nature of realistic multi-stage attack campaigns. In this paper, we introduce an attacker-centric security objective for VMP, where the attack surface is quantified through a sequential adversarial decision process. Using multi-agent reinforcement learning, an attacker agent explores VM placements to exploit critical attack paths, while a defender agent adapts placements to reduce compromise opportunities while maintaining operational efficiency. This learned attack objective provides a more realistic security measure by accounting for network topology, exploit dependencies, and the strategic value of compromised resources. To enable evaluation and reproducibility, we release CyberBattleSim-VMP CyberBattleSim-VMP, a simulation framework supporting configurable attacker and defender behaviors for security-aware VMP research. Experiments in cloud-inspired simulation demonstrate that optimizing VMP against learned attacker behaviors reduces the effective attack surface by an average of 17% (up to 21%) while improving performance by an average of 5% (up to 12%) compared with five heuristic placement strategies under our evaluation protocol.

Position paper Why AI-Assisted Security Monitoring Is Hard to Deploy: A Taxonomy of Policy-to-Operation Gaps in Industrial Control Environments

Jeong-Han Yun1, Jina Kang1, Hyesong Choi2

  • 1The Affiliated Institute of ETRI
  • 2Soongsil University
Read abstractHide abstract

AI-based anomaly detection is often presented as a promising component of security monitoring in industrial control environments, yet field deployment remains difficult. This paper argues that deployment failure can arise not only from insufficient detector accuracy but also from an under-specified deployment layer between high-level AI/security governance and site-specific OT operation. We define the missing layer as a set of policy-to-operation gaps that arise when high-level requirements are not translated into site-specific decisions about unacceptable process consequences, monitored assets and tags, process states, security-relevant threat effects and attacker assumptions, validation data, metrics, roles, lifecycle controls, and evidence obligations. Through a practice-oriented conceptual synthesis of AI-governance, ICS-security, operator-centered ICS, MLOps, and anomaly-detection literature, we propose eight policy-to-operation gaps: site-specific scope, objective mismatch, scenario-to-test-data validation, context integration, performance criteria, role-aware actionability, lifecycle and drift, and evidence reconstruction. We map each gap to its deployment failure mode and a candidate operational artifact, and outline an intended-use-based readiness process that assesses artifact sufficiency according to consequence and permitted use, while distinguishing automatable checks and assistive functions from accountable human decisions. OT is not the only domain in which these gaps arise; rather, it is a high-consequence stress case in which AI-specific, adversarial, and organizational gaps interact visibly and expensively. We derive open research questions and argue for an explicit monitoring-domain profile that declares the conditions under which a detector is considered operationally valid for the declared use and an alarm candidate may be presented as an alarm for escalation or to support security decisions and incident reconstruction. The paper calls for a shift from model-centered anomaly detection to deployment-centered AI-assisted security monitoring that is scoped, adversary-aware, contextualized, actionable, maintainable, and reconstructable.

Research paper AgentLSD: Evaluating AI Security Agents Under Adversarial Task Contamination

Matteo Golinelli1, Idilio Drago2, Matteo Boffa3, Francesco Bergadano2, Bruno Crispo1

  • 1University of Trento
  • 2University of Turin
  • 3Politecnico di Torino
Read abstractHide abstract

AI agents for security inspect web pages, source code, logs, configuration files, and command outputs. These environments may contain deceptive artifacts that influence the agent’s behavior. We call this adversarial task contamination. We introduce Trojan Hippo Bench, a dynamic evaluation framework for persistent memory attacks and memory-layer defenses, comprising two components: (1) an OpenEvolve-based adaptive red-teaming benchmark that stress-tests defenses and memory backends against continuously refined attacks, and (2) a capability-aware security/utility analysis for persistent memory systems, enabling principled reasoning about defense deployment across different usage profiles. Instantiated on an email assistant across four memory backends (explicit tool memory, agentic memory, RAG, and sliding-window context), the undefended Trojan Hippo attack achieves up to 85–100% attack success rate (ASR) against current frontier models from OpenAI and Google, with planted memories successfully activating even after 100 benign sessions. We evaluate four memory-system defenses inspired by basic security principles; they reduce attack success to as low as 0–5%, but at utility costs that vary widely with the task mix. Effective real-world deployment thus remains an open challenge that Trojan Hippo Bench is built to study; we release it to support reuse and extension.

Benchmark paper What Breaks Local Watermarks? A Robustness Benchmark for Local Invisible Image Watermarking

Kai Yao1, Bence Szilágyi2, Sebestyén Kamp2,1, Máté Poór2, Máté Szilveszter2, Matyas K. Zsoldos2, Marc Juarez1

  • 1University of Edinburgh
  • 2Garandor
Read abstractHide abstract

Local image watermarking embeds an invisible signal into selected image regions rather than spreading it across the entire image, enabling payload recovery from specific objects or regions without perceptibly altering the image. Existing studies evaluate the robustness of payload recovery and localization under image transformations, but they often focus on their own proposed method, resulting in narrow evaluations with inconsistent choices of transformations, datasets, and metrics. These inconsistencies across studies limit direct comparisons across methods and muddle the overall picture of local watermark robustness. To address this gap, we present the first systematic robustness benchmark for local watermarks across 55 image transformations, including (i) signal distortions, (ii) changes in image coordinate alignment, (iii) indirect local edits, and (iv) direct watermark edits. The benchmark evaluates MaskWM, WAM, OmniGuard, TrustMark, and PixelSeal, all methods that either provide native localization or require minimal adaptation to support it. Our results show that all evaluated methods are vulnerable to some transformation, with MaskWM standing out as offering the strongest payload recovery and localization, although it has the lowest image quality in the clean setting. Synchronization further improves MaskWM's payload recovery under several geometric transformations, albeit at an additional cost to image quality. A key finding is that local watermark robustness depends strongly on the nature of the transformation: signal distortions are often tolerated by the strongest methods, while geometric misalignment and generative local edits, such as inpainting and outpainting, can completely impair payload recovery. We observe that payload recovery and localization are related but not interchangeable, and both strongly depend on the transformation's impact on the watermark region.

Scope of AISec

Applications of Artificial Intelligence (AI), Machine Learning (ML), and data mining to security and privacy problems are ubiquitous. The analytic tools and intelligent behavior provided by AI/ML models is now pivotal for autonomous real-time analysis and decision-making in domains with a wealth of data or that require quick reactions to constantly changing situations. The use of learning-based methods in security-sensitive domains, in which adversaries may attempt to mislead or evade intelligent machines, creates new frontiers for security research. The acknowledged widespread adoption of deep learning techniques, such as Large-Language Models (LLM), whose security properties are difficult to reason about directly, has only added to the importance of this research. At the same time, and unfortunately, application and deployment of AI/ML techniques often leads to privacy-related issues, such as the possibility to extract sensitive data from trained models, as well as training on sensitive or confidential data.
The AISec workshop provides a venue for presenting and discussing new developments in the intersection of security and privacy with AI and ML.

Submission Guidelines

We invite the following types of papers, all of which are of archival nature and, hence, cannot substantially overlap with papers that have been published previously or that are simultaneously submitted to a journal or conference/workshop with proceedings.

  • Original research papers on any topic in the intersection of AI or machine learning with security, privacy, or related areas.
  • Position and open-problem papers discussing the relationship of AI or machine learning to security or privacy, advocating for new perspectives, or providing a critical look at prior research.
  • Systematization-of-knowledge papers, which should distill the AI or machine learning contributions of a previously-published series of security papers.
  • [NEW] Benchmark papers proposing a new benchmark in the context of AI/ML and security/privacy. (Note: artifact sharing at submission time is required for papers in this category. We also encourage authors to clearly articulate what the artifact contains to facilitate reviewing.)
The authors can specify the paper type in the submission form. We encourage authors to share their artifacts at submission time by providing a link to an (anonymous) repository. We also endorse authors to reflect on the ethical implications of their research, which can be discussed in the paper in a dedicated section or paragraph.

Papers not following the following guidelines will be desk-rejected. Submissions must be in English and properly anonymized. Ensure that there is no way to identify authors, including, e.g., when linking code repositories (consider using anonymous.4open.science), when referencing prior work of the same authors, or in the header of the pages. Submitted papers must be at most 10 pages long in double-column ACM format---excluding bibliography and well-marked appendices, for which up to two additional pages can be used (hence, the paper's overall length must be of 12 pages at most). Committee members are not required to read appendices, so the paper should be intelligible without them. Papers must be submitted as PDF files, and must strictly comply with the ACM format. We point the authors to the main ACM CCS'26 formatting instructions (except with page limits as described above), which can be found here. Benchmark papers without a valid (and functional) link to a resource containing the benchmark will be rejected. Submitted papers must also comply with the "Use of Generative AI" policy described below. Accepted papers will be published in the ACM Digital Library and/or ACM Press.
NOTE: if, when submitting your paper on HotCRP, you get a warning stating that "Too many pages: the limit is 12 pages, found 13", this is OK -- as long as the 13th page contains ONLY the "Generative AI paragraph"

One author of each accepted paper is required to attend the workshop and present the paper for it to be included in the proceedings. All accepted submissions will be presented at the workshop as posters. A fraction of accepted papers will be selected for presentation as spotlights based on their potential impact and significance to the AISec community. Nonetheless, all accepted papers should be considered as having equal importance and will be included in the ACM workshop proceedings.

Use of Generative AI [NEW]

All submissions must have an explicit paragraph (which does not count towards the page limit, and to be put after the references/appendices) declaring whether, and the extent of, Generative AI tools (such as LLMs) have been used in the making of the research paper. This means that even if no AI tools have been used, it should be explicitly stated (e.g., by writing "No AI tools have been used for carrying out this research"). Regardless, using AI tools for research activities (e.g., developing experimental source code, generating new data, or as writing assistants) is allowed, but it must follow the official ACM policies (described here).
At the same time, we will also require all reviewers to explicitly declare if Generative AI tools (such as LLMs) have been used in the reviewing process. We expect reviewers to act responsibly, and in ways that comply with the official ACM policies in this regard. For instance, sharing any part of a paper with some AI tools may break the confidentiality of the peer review.
